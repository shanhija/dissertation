# Dissertation

## The brief

The author's words, as the game was first asked for:

> I want to build a game based on gentleman's dispute. but this is mad doctors that dissertate, i.e., defend their theses, like phd's do, but this time literally. maybe instead of health, the credibility is the "hp" bar, and if you lose your credibility, then you are out for the round.
>
> I'd build it in TypeScript with Three.js and the Rapier physics engine, running in the browser. 
>
> An AI builds the map every time. make sure it has hight differences and functional things, like in gentlemans dispute. 
>
> Instead of weapon stands, a player may stop and write anything they want to have. Opus understands that like in silly-billy game in this repo, but this is for adults, so shooting, slashing etc is ok. The new weapon or object is dropped as a dropbox that descends from the sky. Some other player may steal it also. If you don't want to type, you may press a random object-button, and opus invents a new thing, that is completely new and nothing anyone has got even in a previous match. database of things is controlled later, but between game runs it's maintained. 
>
> opus thinks while the object drops. and it's ready to open when opus is ready. opus designs the object, what it does, how it affects the environment, and how much damage, if anything, a hit does. some of the requests can be simple objects, 
>
> be general in the prompt and not list things it can and can't do. ask what could remotely be possible and try to produce that. riding a shark bicyckle, where hitting the mouth reduces hp, is good. try not to give examples in the prompts, since that directs llm's too much. 
>
> the objective of the game is to do damage and reduce other peoples hp to 0. make sure all objects are somewhat fair, and none can take more than 10 hp at a time. no "everyone else dies" -actions on objects, or "i win".
>
> the game is meant to be silly, so the llm could sometimes work like a traditional genie and try to twist the players words as much as possible. if they aren't specific, choose the unobvious choice. 
>
> perhaps some weapons affect players ability to ask for weapons. like next time the person is asking for a thing, all object words are replaced with the word "duck". or something like that.

## What it is

The brief above is the author's words: a party brawl in the spirit of A Gentlemen's Dispute, played by mad doctors who defend their theses literally, in first person 3D, for adults. Credibility is the health bar; an AI builds every arena; nothing is picked from a weapon stand, a player writes what they want and Opus makes it. Added in chat (2026-11-09): the character editor matters from the start, players dress while Opus builds each round's stage, and they can type how they want to look; a taunt button with silly, never-repeated lines spoken aloud; and things from crates can be disguises too (a box to hide in). How to run and play it is in [`README.md`](README.md).

On 2026-11-11 the user asked for a review of the whole game ("reconsider the whole game: how to make it more visually appealing, especially the 3d objects ... how to improve the voice acting, how to improve the playability, how to make the whole game more coherent and fun, and fair"; then "the objects and the arena look blocky, sharp edges etc. ... perhaps there's a cheap step or design that could improve?" and "cheap step in the design prompt, prebuilding anything can be laborious"), and after it said: "Do all that you suggested, the way you recommend. Also, make boxes only open for requester for the first three seconds, then they can be opened by anyone." Decisions marked "the review" come from that. Its measurements were taken on the development machine, often under load from work running alongside, with bots, fake players and headless browsers; nothing of it has yet been played at a party or heard by ear.

## Decisions

- **Friends at a LAN party; the host's computer is the server.** The game (sim, bots, made code) runs in the dev server; browsers send input and draw snapshots. Only local addresses may connect (loopback, 10/8, 172.16/12, 192.168/16, link-local, fc00::/7). `npm run dev` serves plain http on 3003: no certificate covers a LAN address.
- **The page is ready in under 3 s, not 10** (2026-11-11: "loading the game page takes ages."). Measured in headless Chromium drawing on the host's GTX 1080 (ANGLE on OpenGL, as Brave does), the dev server restarted on its cache with a page coming at once (as after `npm run dev` with a page open), the library copied, no Claude CLI, load 4 to 8, medians of 8 alternating runs (3 of the first); before the review's work (01ea440) / after it (d858343) / now:
  - "Finding the committee…" gone at 2.1 / 2.2 / 1.4 s; the dressing room's first frame at 5.8 / 5.9 / 2.7 s; the page answering input (its last task of 50 ms or more) from 9.9 / 10.3 / 2.7 s. A reload: first frame 3.0 / 2.7 / 1.7 s, answering from 7.1 / 7.3 / 1.7 s. With Vite's cache and the browser's empty: 5.9 / 5.8 / 3.5 s and 10.1 / 10.4 / 3.7 s. The review's work was no slower, but for two more shader programs in the dressing room (15, 0.05 to 0.1 s more waiting on the GPU).
  - The page's thread was busy 9.3 to 9.6 s of the 12 s to answering, now 2.4 of 2.9 s, and the game's start took 2.1 s of the server thread's time in 2.7 s: both bound by their one thread, so with every core of the host busy (load 18 on 12) each would take about half as long again.
  - The surfaces (`scene/materials.ts`) were most of it: the robing room's six painted before the first frame (2 s), the other sixteen right after it, each in one go (up to 470 ms at a time, 4.7 s). A key string made for each of the millions of lattice lookups, and u and v wrapped again in every octave, took 60% of it: all 22 take 2.4 s instead of 5.8, every pixel the same (hashed in Chromium). They are painted ahead from the page's start, the robing room's first, 16 rows at a time in slices of 6 ms, so the page draws and answers between; one wanted before it is done is finished at once.
  - The game loads as the dev server starts listening (`plugin.ts`), not on the first visit, and Vite makes the page's 63 modules (6 deep) then too (`server.warmup`): the first page after a start waited 1.2 s for its modules, made as the browser came to them, and its socket waited for the game, 2 s from the page's first request. A visit after the game's code changed starts the new game, as before.
  - The archive's things are compiled when first drawn: compiling all 81 kept took 0.35 s of the game's start, mostly a `node:vm` context each.
- **Made code runs on the server, behind the guard** (`src/sim/guard.ts`). The guard allows only the code's own names, its parameters and plain JavaScript, refuses constructor and prototype keys, and puts a loop and time budget in every loop and function; each design gets a `node:vm` context of its own. The guard is the wall; the vm only keeps designs apart. Friends' words reach Opus, never the code. A call has 12 ms; a thing's handler run inside another thing's call (a blow knocks a holder down and their thing's `dropped` runs) gets 12 ms of its own, so a slow one is not blamed on the hitter; an arena's build has 1 s for all it builds (below).
- **Up to 8 players; bots only when asked for** (the chair's setting). Bots never ask Opus: they grab crates, get a crate filled from the archive at each round's start (beside their spawn) and every 25 to 45 s, and wear random parts from the wardrobe. A bot waits 1.4 m beside a crate it may not open yet and takes it the moment it may (walking into it, a bot took itself for stuck and never opened it). A bot starts each round afresh: its plans are timed on the sim's clock, which starts again every round, and a bot kept from the round before stood idle where it last stepped round something for as long as that round had lasted.
- **The match** (the review; replaces "first to 5 rounds takes the match"): the chair sets the rounds to win (3, 4, 5 or 7) in the dressing room; left to the room, it is 5 with 2 or 3 doctors, 4 with 4 or 5 and 3 with 6 to 8, bots counted, fixed at the match's first round (first to 5 among 8 equal doctors took a median 19 rounds; first to 3, 9). Only doctors who play count, for this and for a round's end: a page without a name only watches (one open had let a solo player win rounds at once). When the chair sets fewer wins than two doctors already have, the one with the most takes the match. The dressing room and the standings say how many; a round's and a match's end sound a chime. No perks.
- **Harm, enforced in `sim.ts`:** a hit takes at most 10; a thing takes at most its budget from a player in any 20 s, 10 for most and 20 for one hard to hit or risky ("most take just 10, but hard to hit (sniper) can have two rounds or risky (melee) can do 20"; at first the budget was for all its uses, and weapons felt like they had "1-2 charges", so on 2026-11-09 it was made to refill), and the thing prompt says things can be used again and again unless running out is what was asked for; one attacker takes at most 20 a second from another (the arena counts as one); with three or more others in, a thing never reaches all of them within a second. A thrown thing hits for 6 (against its budget).
  - Bare hands are budgeted like a thing (the review): a shove takes 3, at most 10 (`FISTS_BUDGET`) from one player in any 20 s, counted per shover and victim; the push is never budgeted. "A push and 3" was the game's own choice, not the user's, and shoves alone dismissed a doctor in about 19 s; now it takes at least 190. Over 36 bot rounds (the hall and 5 kept arenas, 4 and 8 bots) shoves fell from 37% to 25 or 26% of the credibility taken and things rose from 52 or 53% to 63 to 66%; the median round went from 89 to 104 s with 4 bots and from 92.5 to 101.5 s with 8; the patience ran out in 2 of 36 rounds before and after.
  - Falls are put down to the last blow (the review). Each doctor keeps the last blow from another doctor: harm (even when a budget leaves nothing to take), `w.push`, `w.topple`, `w.blast`, `w.teleport`, fields, taut ropes, a body's momentum, a shove; a move of their own does not replace it. A hard landing costs 2.5 for each m/s over 12 (about 3.5 m of drop is free, 5 m costs about 4, from about 6.4 m up the 10 of one hit; 2026-11-10: "fall damage, yes"), put down to the last blow within 3 s and charged to its thing's budget (or the shover's hands) and the per-second cap; a made thing that makes its holder jump higher raises the free speed by as much, so its own jumps cost nothing. A fall out of the arena puts the doctor back at a spawn point and always costs 10, credited to the last blow within 5 s, outside every budget and cap (under the cap, a doer who had just taken 20 that second knocked someone out for nothing). In bot rounds, 0 of 6 dismissals by a fall out were credited before, 3 of 8 after.
  - The referee says what it did (the review). The hit event names what hit (a thing, a shove, a fall, a fall from the arena, the arena, the committee's patience). When a budget cuts a hit to nothing, a `spent` event says so, at most once per thing and doctor every 2 s, with the seconds until it may take again; the caps say nothing. Over every library thing (12 runs of 25 s against one doctor), the things that took more than twice their budget in 25 s went from 10 to 3; the Gavel still takes about 50 in 25 s by flinging its victims out of the roofless hall.
- **Holds, and one control rule** (holds with the weapons below; the rule from the review): an effect may stop a doctor where they stand (speed 0, jump 0) or bind their hands (nothing used, picked up or thrown). Seconds a doctor cannot play (held still, hands bound, blind 0.7 or more, pulled by a rope, in a field they cannot walk against, or lying knocked flat) count up and free seconds count down. At 5 s (`HOLD_MAX`) they shake free: the holds end, and for 5 s from when they are on their feet no hold takes, ropes and fields let them be, and knocks only push (a blast still reaches them, and only pushes). A ragdoll that runs out while flying is not stood up. `w.held` counts lying flat too, as the thing reference does. Before, a hold ended after 5 s in a row with 2 s of rest and a knockdown was outside it; the longest any library thing kept a victim held or flat in 25 s went from 20.2 s to 15.8 s, and the rule allows 15 s in 25, about half over a long run.
- **Asking:** one crate in the air per player (the crate brought to a defence not counted) and 15 s between requests; a typed request is always designed afresh. T and R work in the countdown too, and when the round goes live everyone's next request waits at least 15 s (the review). A crate opens within 30 s: a design not ready by then leaves the crate to a random thing from the archive, and is kept when it comes; since the review it also takes the archive's thing's place while nobody has opened the crate. A design that fails its trial and goes back to be fixed gives its crate 15 s more (`FIX_GRACE`), since one design in six needed a fix round past 30 s.
- **A crate is its asker's for 3 s** (2026-11-11: "make boxes only open for requester for the first three seconds, then they can be opened by anyone"): a crate that turns ready opens only for its asker for 3 s (`CRATE_OWNER_ONLY`), then for whoever is first. E from anyone else passes over it to whatever else is near, and says whose it is and for how long. A crate ready in the countdown has no ready time until the round goes live (`sim.goLive`), so its 3 s are not spent while nobody can press E. Bots wait beside it. The thing prompt says so.
- **The wish shown** (the review). The genie is rolled as the crate drops. Its label shows the asker and their words (cut to 60 characters, never inside an emoji; "something new" for the button; "from the archive" for a crate filled as it drops) while it comes in and is designed, then "For <name>: N s" to everyone else while it is the asker's alone, then "Open it: E"; in the countdown "For <name>" to everyone, the asker too, since E opens nothing until the round is live. The words stand just over the lid, below the eyes, with the cog that turns while it is made above them: at head height they covered the asker's own name tag to whoever faced them. Nothing marks a twisted crate before it opens (the snapshot never carries the genie). When it opens, the feed tells who took whose crate, what was asked and what came, "The genie twisted it.", "from the archive in place of" the words, and the review it was under; the hand card says how the thing came.
- **Bring something to your defence** (the review). The dossier asks "What will you bring to your defence?" (empty: something nobody has had, with the random button's chance of the aside). Words count only from the dressing room, and the page sends its field whenever it (re)connects, so after a reload the empty field wins. The design starts when the player is Ready, at most two at a time in the break (`BRING_AT_ONCE`, half the four designs Opus was measured to take at once no slower than one, leaving the rest to outfits being made); the last words win while it waits its turn, and a Ready player who left has no turn until they are back and Ready again.
  - At the round's start every doctor gets a crate beside their spawn, dropped after one physics step: Rapier's queries see the arena only after a step, and without it every crate came in on its doctor and shoved them 0.8 m (with it, 2.5 to 4.8 m away in the hall with 8 doctors, and nobody moved). A bot's, and everyone's without the CLI, is filled from the archive at once. A player who was never Ready brings their last words, designed from then on, and a design still waiting its turn starts then too. A crate is filled when its design is ready, or from the archive 30 s into the live round. A failed design says so in the dossier and leaves an archive thing in the crate.
- **Reviews by the dismissed** (the review). Once a round, a dismissed player (or a named one watching) may write a review, at most 200 characters, of the player they watch; never of a bot, which never asks, so the review would never bite. It is laid as a curse by them on that player's next request, shown in the feed, on the slip ("Under review by" the reviewer) and when the crate opens, and logged as `review`. One rule (`noReview` in `game.ts`) serves the message and the page. Every curse reads as a review, a thing's too. It lapses at the round's end.
- **The random button's aside** (the review: all 76 things kept by 2026-11-10 hurt): one random request in three, and one empty brought thing in three, is told "This one takes no credibility from anyone by itself: what it does changes the fight some other way." (`ASIDE`), and it is logged. One general line, no example, no list.
- **Models:** things and looks go to Opus however many are in flight (2026-11-11: "have all go to opus"; before, the fifth and sixth at once went to Sonnet and any more to Haiku). Four at once were measured no slower than one; more are unmeasured. A model is passed over for 2 minutes when a design of it fails, or when two in a row miss their crates. A design counts toward that streak only when a crate waits for it, judged by that crate's own time over the whole wait (a second try included): answered less than 2 s before the crate falls back to the archive. A request's crate waits 30 s, a fix's second try the time left plus `FIX_GRACE`, a thing brought and begun at the round's start the countdown plus 30 s; a thing brought and made in the break, and a look, count toward no streak. Arenas always go to Opus (up to three tries with the checks' problems); taunts and titles to Haiku. All through the CLI driver (`src/server/claude.ts`), `--effort low`.
  - Changed in the review: before, one design over 22 s passed Opus over, and with today's prompt that followed most designs, so most crates went to Sonnet while nobody else was designing. One over 28 s is still no sign of congestion (7 of 20 Opus designs took that long with nothing else running), so it takes two in a row. That streak is now the only sign of congestion the picker reads (the earlier rule: "if the game sees it's becoming congested, then switch to sonnet or haiku online"). Replaying the picker, with the count of designs in flight it read then, at 4 to 8 requests a minute, Opus gets 7 to 13% of crates under the old rule, 19 to 31% with one miss over 28 s, and 56 to 73% with two in a row. Judged against a request's 28 s, two slow outfits or two slow brought things would have passed Opus over for the round's first 2 minutes with no crate missed.
  - At a round's start up to eight brought designs can be in flight, all on Opus, and the requests from go-live + 15 s join them.
- **Things modelled in their real forms** (the review: "the objects and the arena look blocky, sharp edges etc. ... perhaps there's a cheap step or design that could improve?", "cheap step in the design prompt, prebuilding anything can be laborious").
  - The model reference said models were "drawn low-poly and flat-shaded". They are smooth-shaded, and Opus built each part as a box or a cylinder: a median 6 shapes a thing, 71% of them boxes or cylinders, a lathe in 4 of 76 things, and things about twice their real size.
  - The reference now says how a shape is really drawn: a round shape with about a dozen sides round (a tube six); a model's box, the ends of a cylinder and an outline made thick, at its rim and between its points, keep sharp edges; nothing is blended where shapes meet; seen from within, a shape can be invisible, so a surface seen from within needs a thickness of its own. It asks for the real form in its true proportions, each form made with the shape that makes it, and a box only for what really is a block. The arena prompt asks for pieces and decorations modelled in their real forms.
  - Things and worn parts write their form in a sentence or two (`form`) just before the model code; arenas do not (no gain in two runs). The form is neither stored nor logged.
  - Chosen from five approaches, each tried on six asks and an observatory and judged from renders: these words cut things' boxes from 31% to 16% and gave both new observatories a dome seen from inside, and writing the form first is what made the swivel chair catapult a real chair (5 of 5 runs, 0 of 4 without). Things draw about 1.6 times the triangles. Six asks designed with and without the form at the same moment took a median 21.4 s either way (a median 0.2 s longer a pair, a mean 1.8 s); the form streams in 1.0 to 1.7 s; all twelve passed their trial first time; two with the form went past 28 s, none without.
  - Turned down: a separate modelling call (2 of 6 crates past 30 s), and rounded drawing in `mesh.ts` for things and arenas (twice the triangles of every arena, and a change to the renderer, outside the cheap step in the design prompt the user asked for). Rounded drawing is the only option that would also soften the 76 kept things and 49 kept arenas. Worn parts are drawn rounded since 2026-11-11 (Doctors drawn as bodies, below): a doctor wears a handful of parts, an arena has thousands of pieces.
- **The API reference** says every handle is frozen (bodies, sights, zones, fields, ropes, `me` and `w`): 4 of 42 thing designs in the comparison failed their first trial writing a field onto a body. It says `w.hurt` with 0 or less takes nothing and gives nothing back (the Shrink Ray's "heal" never worked).
- **An arena's build has 1 s**, everything it builds included (`BUILD_MS` in `sim/arena.ts`). The 12 ms guard, looked at every 1,024 turns, had stopped sound arenas on a loaded host, and the game silently played a kept one. A build the guard stopped for time is told to build lighter; one that fails for any other reason gets its own error, however long it ran. The 49 kept arenas and 9 new observatories build in 0.4 to 61 ms on a quiet host, up to 232 ms with every core busy and 344 ms with twice as many busy threads as cores; with 1 s all passed under both loads.
- **The voice (2026-11-10):** Kokoro-82M (Apache-2.0, ONNX fp32 through onnxruntime-node, phonemes by espeak-ng) because it is small enough for the host's CPU, needs no Python, has 28 English voices to mix and runs from `npm install` alone; more expressive models (Chatterbox, 0.5B, an exaggeration knob but one voice without reference recordings; NeuTTS-2E) need a GPU or Python to be quick. Measured on the development machine (i7-8750H, no CUDA libraries): a 6 s line takes 1.9 s on 4 threads (fp16 and q8 no faster, more threads no faster), so each player's next taunt is said ahead of V. onnxruntime holds its thread for the whole line (a 5 ms timer did not fire once in 1.6 s), so it runs in a worker thread. Kokoro's style is 128 numbers of timbre and 128 of prosody: Bella's prosody on George's timbre spoke at Bella's pitch (192 Hz against George's 142), Nicole's made speech slower and breathier, Santa's swung wider (5.6 semitones of spread against 3.8). Pushing a voice's prosody past itself (away from the mean) only slowed it, so tones pull toward other voices instead. espeak-ng's `en-gb` does not switch back from `en-us` within one process; `en` does. The men, said with English phonemes: Lewis 81 Hz, Onyx 88, Echo 109, Puck 113, Michael 114, Fable 114, Daniel 122, Liam 126, Fenrir 137, George 142, Santa 150, Eric 160 (Adam is graded F and left out). The women: Alloy 143, Nicole 151 (a whisper), Kore 155, Nova 164, Sky 166, River 178, Emma 180 (the flattest, 2.3 semitones of spread), Aoede 180, Isabella 197, Bella 198, Sarah 198, Heart 205, Lily 205, Jessica 209, Alice 211. Women's prosody on a man's timbre lifts him toward a woman's pitch (Emma's by 35 Hz), so each tone pulls toward voices of the doctor's own sex, but hushed, which takes Nicole's slow breathy delivery for both (about 10 Hz up for a man).
- **No widening, no tremble; Kokoro stays** (2026-11-11). After listening to Kokoro samples on a listening page, the user said: "the voices are, quite flat. considering the docs are fighting", then "these are still flat and autotune-like features stand out in the voice". Heard side by side with variants, they chose: "let's go with No widening, no tremble".
  - The autotune-like sound was the widening: every voiced moment's pitch moved 1.5 to 2.2 times as far from its phrase's trend (each tone's swing) by TD-PSOLA after Kokoro, within 9 semitones up and 8 down of the line's middle note (without that limit a gloating man's highs reached 330 Hz). At a swing of 1.8 George with Onyx went from 93..148 Hz (10th to 90th percentile) to 80..171 and Isabella with Kore from 157..242 to 135..299, the voice measurably a little less clean (periodicity 0.88 to 0.85). Kokoro's own style can widen the melody too (George with Onyx pushed from Daniel toward Santa: 3.5 to 6.6 semitones of spread), but it lifts the whole voice with it (123 to 150 Hz).
  - Taken out for good: the widening, the pitch tracking it needed, each tone's swing, `MELODY` and `NOTES`, and the age's tremble (the playback rate wavering about 1% five times a second). Models, arena surfaces, doctors, crates, plain bodies and sights all use it; one program serves them all. `harmonize` pulls colours into an oil painting's range in OKLab (lightness 0.17 to 0.9, chroma at most about 0.12, glow 0.2): every model's colours once when built, solid colours, the doctor's look, effects.
  - The finishing grade (`post.ts`) after tone mapping, on every quality: a little less colour, shadows toward a cool umber-blue and highlights toward cream (the shadow side's hue, as a painter shifts it), a soft curve, darker corners, a canvas weave. The finishing passes are always on now (multisampled above low quality).
  - Effects: clouds (smoke, dust, fire, trails) are flat-brush dabs turned their own way, with bristles, ragged ends and three tones, crisp-edged for smoke and soft for fire; bits are short strokes along their flight on the screen, longer the faster.
